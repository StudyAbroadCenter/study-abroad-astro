# English Localisation Specification

## 1. Status

Phase: **English Localisation — ACTIVE**

Japanese Master MVP: **FIXED**

The Japanese site is not to be casually re-edited during English work. Return to Japanese only for a verified factual correction, a genuine defect, an approved canonical-data update, or an explicitly approved master-design change.

## 2. Purpose

The English site is not an English copy of the Japanese site.

It is the English-language edition of the same Study Abroad product experience, built from:

1. the approved Japanese information architecture and visual hierarchy,
2. verified canonical programme facts,
3. controlled terminology and official names,
4. English-native editorial judgement.

The English edition must feel intentionally written for international readers.

## 3. Primary Audiences

### 3.1 Individual students

Prospective international university students comparing short-term programmes in Japan.

They need to understand quickly:

- what the programme is,
- who it is for,
- how long it lasts,
- where it takes place,
- what they will study and do,
- whether they are eligible,
- what it costs,
- when and how to apply.

### 3.2 Overseas university staff

Study abroad advisers, international education staff, faculty members, and programme coordinators.

This audience is especially important on Custom Programs and institutional information pages.

They need clarity, credibility, scope, flexibility, and a realistic understanding of what can be discussed or arranged.

## 4. Voice System

English uses three controlled voices.

### A. Student-facing marketing/editorial voice

Use for hero copy, programme discovery, programme benefits, campus-life context, student experience, and student-facing CTAs.

Tone:

- warm,
- concise,
- confident,
- contemporary,
- lightly energetic,
- specific rather than promotional.

Do not sound like a travel advertisement.

### B. Institutional voice

Use for Custom Programs, university-facing explanations, programme design, collaboration, and contact guidance.

Tone:

- professional,
- calm,
- collaborative,
- specific,
- internationally readable.

Avoid sales language such as `bespoke solutions`, `world-class`, or `transformative partnership` unless a specific verified reason supports it.

### C. Rules-and-facts voice

Use for dates, fees, eligibility, accommodation, refunds, deadlines, application steps, and programme conditions.

Tone:

- direct,
- literal,
- neutral,
- scan-friendly.

No wordplay. No persuasive phrasing. No ambiguity.

## 5. Core Editorial Rule

**Translate meaning. Rewrite communication. Preserve facts.**

Do not start from the Japanese sentence and search for English equivalents word by word.

Instead:

1. identify the section's job,
2. identify the verified facts it must carry,
3. identify the reader's likely question,
4. write the clearest natural English answer,
5. fit that answer into the existing visual hierarchy without forcing Japanese line breaks.

## 6. Headings

English headings should normally be shorter than Japanese explanatory headings.

Prefer concrete nouns and active constructions.

Good direction:

- `Find your program`
- `Study Japanese in Kyoto`
- `A focused winter program`
- `Who can apply`
- `What you'll study`
- `Life beyond the classroom`
- `Designed with your university`
- `Plan a custom program`

Avoid generic headings such as:

- `A Unique Experience`
- `Transform Your Future`
- `Discover Japan`
- `Global Learning`
- `An Unforgettable Journey`

unless the section genuinely requires such wording and it is not being used as filler.

## 7. Hero Copy

Hero copy must communicate the page proposition within a few seconds.

A hero may be recomposed from the Japanese version. It does not need matching sentence count or line breaks.

Hero requirements:

- one clear primary idea,
- no unsupported superlative,
- no vague promise,
- no unqualified historical number,
- no implication that students choose a campus where they do not,
- CTA wording that tells the user what happens next.

Do not force a Japanese wordplay concept into English.

For example, a Japanese idea equivalent to `2週間だから 行ける` should be rewritten around the actual value of a short format, not literally translated as `Because it is two weeks, you can go`.

Possible English directions may include:

- `A focused two weeks in Japan`
- `Two weeks. A lot to take home.`
- `Make two weeks count.`

The final choice must fit the actual programme and factual context.

## 8. CTA Style

Use specific verbs.

Preferred patterns:

- `Explore programs`
- `View program details`
- `Check eligibility`
- `See dates and fees`
- `How to apply`
- `Start your application`
- `Contact us`
- `Discuss a custom program`

Avoid vague CTAs such as:

- `Learn more` when a more specific action is available,
- `Discover more`,
- `Get started` when the next step is not obvious,
- `Join us` when this could imply acceptance or enrolment.

## 9. Programme Finder

The English Program Finder should help students compare programmes without overstating incomplete or unverified details.

Rules:

- verified facts may be displayed directly,
- provisional or unverified fields must not be rewritten as confirmed facts,
- compact descriptors may be editorially localised,
- comparison labels must be consistent across programmes,
- unavailable data should be handled explicitly rather than guessed.

The Finder should answer practical comparison questions before selling the experience.

## 10. Programme Pages

Programme pages should normally answer in this order:

1. What is this programme?
2. Who is it for?
3. When and where does it run?
4. What will I study/do?
5. What does it cost?
6. Where will I stay, if applicable?
7. Am I eligible?
8. How do I apply?
9. What conditions or deadlines matter?

The approved Japanese layout remains the baseline, but English copy may reorder explanatory sentences within a section when needed for clarity.

## 11. Custom Programs

Custom Programs is B2B content.

Do not write it as if an individual student can request a personal itinerary.

The English page should make clear that Ritsumeikan works with overseas universities and educational institutions to discuss short-term group programmes around objectives such as:

- academic themes,
- programme length,
- participant profile,
- class content,
- cultural activities,
- student exchange,
- fieldwork,
- campus and facilities,
- accommodation and operational feasibility.

Use collaborative wording such as:

- `We work with universities and educational institutions to design short-term group programs in Japan.`
- `Program content and delivery are discussed with each partner institution.`
- `Campus selection depends on the academic purpose, schedule, facilities, accommodation, and other operational requirements.`

Do not promise that every requested element can be provided.

## 12. Support Language

Where the Japanese site states that the Study Abroad Support Desk can provide support in English, preserve that level of claim.

Preferred direction:

`The Study Abroad Support Desk can assist students in English.`

Do not strengthen this into claims such as:

- `Fully bilingual support`
- `English support is available at all times`
- `All services are provided in English`

unless separately verified.

## 13. Participation Evidence

Historical participation data must include its scope and period.

Do not place numbers such as `522 participants` in a hero without context.

Country/region counts refer to the location of participating universities/institutions, not participant nationality.

Historical participation must not be described as a current partnership network.

## 14. Programme Names and Controlled English

Use official English programme names exactly where confirmed.

For RWJP 2027:

- Official name: `Ritsumeikan Winter Japanese Program`

Do not invent alternate official names for branding consistency.

Official campus and university names should follow the controlled glossary once established.

## 16. Dates, Fees, and Number Formatting

Use English-readable date formats that avoid ambiguity.

Preferred page prose:

- `January 12–February 11, 2027`
- `September 30–October 14, 2026`

Use ISO values in canonical data where possible, and format for display by locale.

For Japanese yen in English prose, prefer consistent forms such as:

- `JPY 370,000`
- `JPY 92,500`

Do not mix `¥370,000`, `370,000 yen`, and `JPY 370,000` across comparable fact modules without a deliberate style decision.

## 17. English Typography and Layout

The Japanese design hierarchy is the baseline, not a fixed text geometry.

English commonly requires different line lengths and wrapping.

Allow:

- different line breaks,
- slightly different content widths,
- button width adjustment,
- card-height adjustment,
- different heading measure,
- locale-specific font stack if needed.

Do not solve English overflow by shrinking text below the established readable hierarchy.

Check at minimum:

- 320px,
- 390px,
- tablet,
- desktop.

## 18. Punctuation and Style

Use normal English punctuation.

Do not copy the Japanese convention of removing punctuation from marketing headings when English punctuation would improve readability.

Use sentence case for most headings and controls unless the established design deliberately requires another treatment.

Avoid excessive ampersands, slashes, title case, all caps, and decorative punctuation.

## 19. Spelling and International English

Use clear international English suitable for a global university audience.

Official names take precedence over house spelling preferences.

Where no official form controls the word, use one consistent spelling style across the site. Do not mix variants casually.

## 20. Metadata

Every English route requires English-language:

- page title,
- meta description,
- Open Graph text where used,
- canonical URL under `/en/`,
- `lang="en"`,
- `hreflang` links to published equivalents.

Do not translate Japanese metadata literally if a more natural search-facing description is available.

## 21. Accessibility

English alt text must describe the image naturally for the page context.

Do not translate Japanese alt text word for word when it produces unnatural English.

Controls and links must describe their action clearly without relying on nearby visual context.

## 22. English Visual UAT

English publication requires the same seriousness as Japanese Visual UAT.

Check:

- HTTP errors,
- JavaScript errors,
- local-resource 404s,
- horizontal overflow,
- heading wrapping,
- small-text warnings,
- navigation fit,
- CTA fit,
- card balance,
- footer wrapping,
- screenshots at controlled widths.

English-specific failures include technically valid layouts that look obviously translated because of awkward wrapping, excessive label length, or forced Japanese line structure.

## 23. Native Readability Audit

Before merge, review each English page for:

- translated syntax,
- unnatural noun chains,
- unnecessary passive voice,
- vague marketing language,
- repeated generic study-abroad phrases,
- inconsistent programme terminology,
- bureaucratic wording,
- unexplained Japanese institutional concepts,
- factual strengthening introduced for fluency.

A page may pass build and Visual UAT and still fail this editorial gate.

## 24. Page Delivery Order

English implementation order:

1. shared terminology / controlled glossary
2. shared locale and route foundation
3. home
4. Program Finder
5. RWJP
6. RWJP Express
7. Custom Programs
8. RSJP / RDSP / RBMP and other programme pages as facts allow
9. About

11. Contact
12. 404
13. metadata / hreflang / sitemap verification
14. English Visual UAT
15. Native Readability Audit
16. cross-language factual audit
17. merge decision

## 25. Definition of Done

English V1 is complete only when:

- required English routes exist,
- every factual statement is traceable to approved content/canonical facts,
- English reads naturally rather than as translated Japanese,
- headings and CTAs are idiomatic,
- programme terminology is consistent,
- metadata is localised,
- navigation works,
- 320px/390px/tablet/desktop layouts pass,
- no accidental Japanese prose remains,
- no provisional information has been promoted into fact,
- cross-language fact audit passes.

## 26. Final Rule

**The Japanese site tells us what the product means. Canonical data tells us what is true. English localisation decides how an English-speaking reader should naturally receive that meaning and truth.**
